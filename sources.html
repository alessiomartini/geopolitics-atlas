Sources & Methodology

Every claim on this site is expected to be traceable to a cited source. This page documents how sources are classified and how that classification governs which claims they may support.

Two independent axes

Tier (below) measures editorial reliability: how rigorous is an outlet's fact-checking and correction culture, and how editorially independent is it, in general, from state or factional control.

Source type measures epistemological distance from the event itself:

  • Primary — original material: treaties, official statements, court/tribunal rulings, satellite imagery, raw datasets (casualty counts, event logs), first-hand on-scene reporting.
  • Secondary — analysis or interpretation of primary material: most news journalism, think-tank analysis.
  • Tertiary — compilations of secondary material: encyclopedic overviews, background explainers.
  • Quaternary — indexes or reviews of tertiary material: literature reviews, meta-analyses. Rarely cited directly on this site.

A source's tier is fixed in the registry below. Its type (primary vs. secondary) is usually fixed too, but its independence — third-party vs. involved — is not: it depends on which conflict is being discussed. A national broadcaster is a third party for a war on the other side of the world, and an involved party for one involving its own government. Every citation on a conflict page must make that explicit inline (e.g. "Kyiv, official statement" or "Moscow, TASS") rather than relying on this page alone.

Citation rules

  • The more consequential the claim, the higher the bar. High-stakes claims — casualty counts, atrocity allegations, territorial control, attack attribution — require Tier A–C sourcing, and preferably two or more independent (third-party) sources in agreement. If sources disagree, both figures are shown with their sources rather than one being silently picked.
  • Background facts (dates, geography, treaty text, population figures) may cite a single Tier A–D source.
  • Involved-party claims (a government's or faction's own account) are always attributed explicitly by name. They appear in "Current State of Facts" only when corroborated by a third-party source; otherwise they belong in "Contested Narratives / Common Myths," labeled as that party's claim.
  • Tier E sources are never used to establish a fact — only to accurately represent what a state-controlled or partisan outlet is saying, which is itself sometimes the relevant fact (e.g. "Russian state media described the strike as...").
