# Kashmir Dispute

## Historical Context

When British India was partitioned in 1947 into India and Pakistan, the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir — ruled by a Hindu maharaja over a Muslim-majority population — was left to choose which country to join. After militias from Pakistan entered the territory, the maharaja signed an Instrument of Accession to India on 26 October 1947, on the understanding, per Governor-General Lord Mountbatten, that the accession would be confirmed by a future plebiscite. War between India and Pakistan followed, ending with a UN-mediated ceasefire in 1949 that left the territory divided by a Line of Control, with India administering the larger, more populous portion and Pakistan administering a western strip. UN Security Council Resolution 47 (1948) called for a plebiscite on the region's future after preconditions including troop withdrawal, but disagreement between India and Pakistan over the sequencing of demilitarization meant the plebiscite was never held.

India and Pakistan fought further wars over Kashmir in 1965 and 1999, and China separately administers Aksai Chin, a portion of the original princely state, which India also claims. In August 2019, India's government revoked Article 370 of its constitution, which had granted Jammu and Kashmir special semi-autonomous status, and reorganized the region into two federally administered territories — a move India's government presented as full integration and Pakistan condemned as illegal. India's Supreme Court upheld the revocation in December 2023.

## Current State of Facts

On 22 April 2025, gunmen killed 26 people, mostly tourists, at Baisaran near Pahalgam in Indian-administered Kashmir — the deadliest attack on civilians in India since 2008 ([Human Rights Watch](https://www.hrw.org/news/2025/04/30/deadly-attack-tourists-jammu-and-kashmir)). India attributed the attack to a Pakistan-based militant group and responded on 7 May 2025 with air and missile strikes on sites in Pakistan and Pakistan-administered Kashmir under the codename "Operation Sindoor"; Pakistan responded with strikes of its own, and the two militaries exchanged fire for several days, including aerial engagements, before a ceasefire was announced on 10 May 2025 following U.S.-facilitated diplomacy ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/5/2/pahalgam-attack-a-simple-guide-to-the-kashmir-conflict)). India separately put the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty governing shared river resources with Pakistan into abeyance after the attack, and has since stated it will not restore the treaty while it says Pakistan continues to support cross-border militancy ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/6/22/india-says-it-will-never-restore-indus-waters-treaty-with-pakistan)). In late July 2025, Indian security forces said they had killed the last of the suspects believed responsible for the Pahalgam attack ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/7/29/rebels-behind-kashmir-attack-that-led-to-pakistan-tensions-killed-india)).

The ceasefire has held since May 2025 but the International Crisis Group describes the situation as one of "armed coexistence" rather than resolved tension, with intermittent Line of Control friction and periodic militant attacks continuing to sustain the conflict at a lower level ([International Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/asia-pacific/south-asia/india-pakistan-kashmir)).

On the water dispute, a five-member tribunal at the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague ruled on 31 August 2026 that the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty remains binding and that India had no justification for suspending it, and ordered India to limit construction on the Ratle hydroelectric plant until 90 days after a World Bank-appointed neutral expert decides, expected by July 2027. India's Ministry of External Affairs "categorically" rejected the verdict, calling the body a "so-called Court of Arbitration" with "no jurisdiction whatsoever" over India's sovereign decisions; Pakistan welcomed the interim measures ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/31/india-rejects-hague-court-order-to-restore-indus-waters-pact-with-pakistan)). The Free Press Journal (India) independently dates the award and interim order to 31 August 2026, reports that the tribunal found India's April 2025 "abeyance" does not legally suspend or terminate the treaty, that the Ratle restrictions on certain concreting work last until shortly after the Neutral Expert's decision (expected around July 2027), and that India's official statement called the tribunal "illegally constituted" with decisions "null and void" and kept the suspension in force until Pakistan ends support for cross-border terrorism ([Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/india/india-rejects-hague-arbitration-award-insists-indus-waters-treaty-remains-suspended)); that article does not mention the 90-day period, which therefore rests on Al Jazeera alone. The article gives no enforcement mechanism for the order.

In Pakistan-administered Kashmir, authorities banned the Joint Awami Action Committee (JAAC), a traders' and civil-society movement demanding abolition of 12 assembly seats reserved for refugees from Indian-administered Kashmir, under anti-terrorism law on 5 June 2026; clashes followed, starting with Rawalakot on 8 June. Death-toll figures differ: regional officials reported 11 dead on 8 June; Amnesty International said on 28 July that, according to media reports, at least 40 people had been killed (34 protesters and six police and paramilitary personnel; Amnesty cites these as media figures rather than a verified count, and says mobile internet had been suspended since 5 June, hindering verification) ([Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/07/pakistan-authorities-must-lift-communications-blackout-following-reports-of-lethal-force-used-against-protesters-amid-elections/)); residents and local estimates put the toll above 50, and protest leaders above 60, figures officials have not confirmed ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/3/pakistan-administered-kashmir-elections-and-protests-all-you-need-to-know)). The Pakistan-administered Kashmir legislative election was held in phases from 27 July to 10 August amid a boycott call by the banned group and restricted internet access; early results from the first phase favoured the PML-N of Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif (same source). Final results were not verified here.

In August 2026, U.S. ambassador to India Sergio Gor, on a visit to Srinagar and Ladakh, described Indian-administered Kashmir as "an important part of India"; Pakistan's Foreign Ministry summoned the U.S. chargé d'affaires and said the remarks were factually incorrect and contradicted Washington's longstanding position that Kashmir is disputed ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/20/why-a-trump-envoys-kashmir-visit-has-led-to-us-pakistan-spat)). Pakistani analysts quoted by Al Jazeera did not read the remarks as a policy shift.

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

This dispute is best understood as three distinct positions rather than two. **India's government** holds that Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh included, are an integral and inalienable part of India by virtue of the 1947 Instrument of Accession, that the matter is a closed internal affair, and that unrest in the region is fueled by Pakistan-sponsored cross-border terrorism rather than genuine self-determination sentiment — a position India's Ministry of External Affairs restates in response to nearly every Pakistani or international statement referencing Kashmir. **Pakistan's government** holds that Kashmir's final status remains legally undetermined pending the plebiscite promised under UN Security Council resolutions, refers to Indian-administered Kashmir as "Indian Illegally Occupied Jammu and Kashmir," and accuses India of human rights violations against the Kashmiri population, particularly since the 2019 revocation of Article 370 ([Pakistan Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://mofa.gov.pk/jammu-and-kashmir-dispute?mission=hanoi)). **Kashmiri self-determination and separatist movements**, historically represented by groupings such as the All Parties Hurriyat Conference, hold a position distinct from both states: that the people of Jammu and Kashmir themselves — not New Delhi or Islamabad — should decide the region's future via the plebiscite promised in 1948, with outcomes ranging from full independence to accession to Pakistan depending on the faction. India designates several separatist and militant organizations operating under this banner as unlawful; some Hurriyat-linked figures have themselves split over whether to prioritize independence, accession to Pakistan, or greater autonomy within India.

**Attribution for specific attacks**, including Pahalgam, is also contested: India's government publicly attributed the 2025 attack to a Pakistan-based group, a claim Pakistan's government has denied, calling for an independent or India-led investigation to substantiate the allegation before any retaliatory framing is accepted.
