# Iran-Israel Shadow War

## Historical Context

Iran and Israel were tacit regional partners before Iran's 1979 Islamic Revolution, after which the new government adopted explicit hostility toward Israel and began building a network of allied militias — most importantly Lebanon's Hezbollah, founded in 1982, and later Hamas and other Palestinian factions — that Israel and Western governments describe as Iran's "Axis of Resistance." For decades the two states avoided direct confrontation, instead waging a "shadow war" of covert operations: Israel has been widely linked to assassinations of Iranian nuclear scientists and to cyberattacks, most notably the 2010 Stuxnet virus that damaged centrifuges at Iran's Natanz enrichment site, while Iran armed and funded proxy forces against Israel and, at times, US regional interests. This careful balance broke down after Hamas's 7 October 2023 attack on Israel: Hezbollah opened a support front from Lebanon, and in 2024 Israel killed most of Hezbollah's senior leadership, including Hassan Nasrallah, prompting Iran to launch its first-ever direct missile and drone attacks on Israeli territory in April and October 2024. Diplomacy over Iran's nuclear program collapsed in mid-2025, and on 13 June 2025 Israel launched a large-scale air campaign against Iranian nuclear, military, and leadership targets, killing senior IRGC commanders and nuclear scientists; the US struck Iranian nuclear sites directly on 22 June before a ceasefire took hold on 24 June 2025, in what became known as the "Twelve-Day War" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/12-Day-War)).

## Current State of Facts

Tensions resumed sharply in early 2026. On 28 February 2026, Israel and the United States launched a further joint military campaign against Iran; Israeli and US officials, along with Iranian state media, confirmed that Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed in the strikes, alongside senior military and nuclear officials ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2026/03/01/iran-succession-supreme-leader-khamenei/)). Iran's Assembly of Experts named Khamenei's son, Mojtaba Khamenei, as the new Supreme Leader on 8 March 2026 ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/03/01/middleeast/iran-new-supreme-leader-khamenei-dead-intl-latam)). Fighting, including Iranian strikes on US and regional targets and disruption to shipping near the Strait of Hormuz, continued for over five weeks before a ceasefire took effect in early April 2026. On 17 June 2026, the US and Iran signed a 14-point memorandum of understanding setting a 60-day framework for talks on the nuclear program, sanctions relief, and Strait of Hormuz navigation ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10637/)). That framework has been repeatedly strained: in July 2026 Iran struck commercial vessels it said had bypassed an approved shipping route, and Iran's parliament has moved to curtail cooperation with the International Atomic Energy Agency. IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi has said inspectors have had no access to Iran's declared enrichment sites since February 2026 and cannot verify the current location or status of Iran's enriched-uranium stockpile ([reporting on IAEA statements](https://thehill.com/policy/international/5832414-iran-nuclear-program-iaea-concerns/)). As of November 2026 there is no deal, and the situation has hardened rather than settled. The June memorandum unravelled in July amid renewed confrontation over shipping routes, and US-Iran talks were reported in deadlock in August ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/13/us-iran-talks-in-deadlock-whats-the-latest)). A US naval blockade of Iranian ports and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz became the central bargaining issues: at the UN General Assembly in late September Iran presented a plan to halt fighting on all fronts (including Lebanon), end the blockade and oil sanctions, release frozen assets and reopen the strait after seven days, followed by nuclear talks, and Trump rejected it; on 7 October Vice President JD Vance said Iran must make a "meaningful" cut in enrichment capacity, which analysts read as a possible softening of the earlier US demand that Iran abandon enrichment entirely ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/7/us-sets-new-demands-for-iran-deal-what-are-they)). Gulf oil flows excluding Iran were above 81 percent of pre-war levels in September according to the analytics firm Kpler, as cited there, while attacks on vessels continued. In early November the US confirmed strikes on an Iranian anti-aircraft facility; Iranian media said Qeshm Island was hit, and Iran's security council secretary Mohsen Rezaei said Washington's reply to Iran's seven conditions fell short. Qatar's foreign ministry spokesperson said both sides "remain committed to the negotiation process" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/4/iran-says-us-reply-to-hormuz-plan-unconvincing-trump-confirms-strikes)). These events are fast-moving and the picture may already have changed.

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**How much damage was done to Iran's nuclear program.** After the June 2025 strikes, the Trump administration and Israeli officials, including the Israel Atomic Energy Commission, said Iran's nuclear capability had been "obliterated" or set back by years. A US Defense Intelligence Agency assessment, reported by multiple outlets, instead concluded the strikes set the program back only a few months, and the Pentagon's own public estimate settled around one to two years ([Al Jazeera reporting on US assessment](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/7/3/us-says-its-strikes-degraded-irans-nuclear-programme-by-one-to-two-years)). IAEA chief Grossi has said the agency saw no evidence of a "structured" Iranian weapons program prior to the strikes but has also warned of "major issues" and unresolved questions that could persist. No independent, on-the-ground verification of remaining Iranian nuclear material or capacity has been possible given restricted IAEA access.

**Early-November strikes and shipping incidents.** President Trump called the early-November strike a "minor attack" that "wasn't war", and US officials told US media it was "defensive" after Iranian fire at an American aircraft. Iran's Revolutionary Guard (IRGC) claimed four oil tankers were destroyed after hitting mines in a prohibited lane and that it shot down a US MQ-9 drone, its 56th; these are the parties' own claims, and Al Jazeera noted it could not independently verify whether the IRGC and UKMTO-reported incidents were linked ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/4/iran-says-us-reply-to-hormuz-plan-unconvincing-trump-confirms-strikes)).

**Legality of killing Khamenei.** The US and Israeli governments have described the February 2026 strike on Khamenei as a legitimate wartime action against a head of state directing hostilities, without publicly detailing a specific legal justification. International law scholars are split: some argue a sitting head of state directing an active military conflict can be a lawful target, while others, writing in venues such as Just Security and Lawfare, argue the strike represents an unprecedented breach of the longstanding international norm against targeted assassination of state leaders, warning of a dangerous precedent. Iran's government and state media, including Press TV, have condemned the killing as an act of illegal aggression and vowed retaliation.

**Who bears responsibility for the collapse of diplomacy.** US and Israeli officials characterize the resort to military action as a last resort after Iran refused to curb uranium enrichment and stalled negotiations. Iranian officials counter that Israeli and US strikes — beginning in June 2025, while talks were reportedly still active — demonstrated bad faith and made subsequent Iranian distrust of further negotiation, including reduced IAEA cooperation, a rational response rather than a provocation.
