# Korean Peninsula

## Historical Context

Korea was divided at the 38th parallel by the United States and the Soviet Union following Japan's defeat in 1945, hardening into two separate states in 1948: the Republic of Korea (South) and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North). North Korea invaded the South in June 1950, beginning the Korean War, in which a U.S.-led United Nations Command backed the South and China intervened on the North's side after U.S. forces approached the Chinese border. The war ended not with a peace treaty but with an armistice signed on 27 July 1953 by the UN Command, North Korea and China; South Korea's government did not sign it. The armistice fixed a Military Demarcation Line close to the original 38th-parallel boundary and created the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), a roughly 4-kilometer-wide buffer that remains one of the most heavily fortified borders in the world. Because no peace treaty followed, North and South Korea have remained formally at war for over seven decades.

Decades of intermittent inter-Korean engagement — including summits in 2000, 2007 and 2018 — coexisted with recurring crises over North Korea's nuclear weapons program, which it has pursued since the 1990s despite the Six-Party Talks (2003–2008) and later direct U.S.-North Korea diplomacy under the Trump administration, including a February 2019 Hanoi summit that ended without an agreement over the sequencing of denuclearization steps and sanctions relief.

## Current State of Facts

In January 2024, Kim Jong Un declared that peaceful reunification was no longer a goal of North Korean policy, redefining South Korea as a "hostile state" and ordering the removal of symbols of the reunification project; North Korea has since revised its constitution to reflect a "two hostile states" doctrine rather than the earlier framework of eventual unification ([International Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/cmt/asia-pacific/korean-peninsula/korean-reunification-abandoned-or-merely-deferred)). North Korea has continued expanding its missile arsenal through 2026, including tests of nuclear-capable short-range ballistic missiles with cluster-munition warheads in April 2026 ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/09/g-s1-116848/north-korea-says-its-latest-weapons-tests-included-missiles-with-cluster-bomb-warheads)) and ballistic missile launches from its Sinpo area later that month ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/19/north-korea-launches-ballistic-missiles-towards-sea-off-its-east-coast)).

Tensions rose again in autumn 2026. On 3 October North Korea tested what Al Jazeera described as a hypersonic intermediate-range missile (North Korean state media called it an intermediate-range strategic missile used to train troops on a "hypersonic strategic weapon system", according to [The Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/foreignaffairs/northkorea/20261012/north-korea-fires-multiple-ballistic-missiles-adding-to-streak-of-weapons-tests)); on 12 October it fired multiple ballistic missiles toward the sea off its east coast. South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff said one missile launched from the Pyongyang area flew more than 900 km and declined to say whether the weapons were hypersonic pending further analysis ([The Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/foreignaffairs/northkorea/20261012/north-korea-fires-multiple-ballistic-missiles-adding-to-streak-of-weapons-tests)), while Japan's Deputy Defence Minister Takashi Koyari said at least two missiles were launched 17 minutes apart, the second flying over 910 km on an "irregular" path that he suggested could indicate a hypersonic weapon ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/12/north-korea-fires-multiple-ballistic-missiles-towards-the-sea)). Reuters reported that Kim Jong Un had opened a new defence museum displaying the Hwasong-20 intercontinental ballistic missile, beside a map showing ranges that reach the U.S. mainland ([Reuters via Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/world/asia/north-korea-kim-showcases-missile-us-mainland)).

On the border, three South Korean soldiers were injured in explosions near the Military Demarcation Line in the western DMZ in late September. Seoul blamed landmines laid by the North and demanded an apology; Pyongyang rejected the accusation, and on 23 October South Korea invited the North to military talks, to be arranged through Panmunjom, aimed at mutually confirming the exact location of the line and restoring military communication lines ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/23/south-korea-invites-north-for-military-talks-over-border), [AP via NY1](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/ap-top-news/2026/10/23/south-korea-proposes-military-talks-with-north-korea-to-calm-rising-border-tensions0)); AP dates the blasts to 21 September, says the North denied responsibility and rejected Seoul's demands for an apology and the removal of its remaining mines, and adds that Seoul is open to three-way talks including the U.S.-led UN Command. Al Jazeera reported earlier that a joint South Korean–UN investigation had concluded that North Korean forces planted two mines about a year earlier ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/12/north-korea-fires-multiple-ballistic-missiles-towards-the-sea)); this is the conclusion of the investigating side. Kim Jong Un's sister Kim Yo Jong said new military units had moved into "firing positions" along the border and called the idea of bilateral talks "the climax of the farce"; as of the last report read, Pyongyang had not answered the talks proposal.

On 24 October Kim made an unannounced one-day trip to the Chinese border city of Dandong across the newly opened New Yalu River Bridge. According to Xinhua, as reported by Al Jazeera, he paid tribute at a Korean War memorial to Chinese soldiers and toured a car factory; he was accompanied by Wang Huning, China's fourth-ranking official, who conveyed greetings from Xi Jinping, and no meeting with Xi was reported ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/24/north-koreas-kim-jong-un-arrives-in-china-south-korean-media-says), [AP via News4Jax](https://www.news4jax.com/news/world/2026/10/24/north-korean-leader-kim-jong-un-visits-china-south-korean-news-agency-reports/)). Analysts and South Korea's spy agency believe he may have conveyed Pyongyang's position on possible talks with Donald Trump to Beijing, and there is speculation that the two leaders could meet at the APEC summit in China in November; no meeting has been confirmed ([Al Jazeera](https://aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/31/us-south-korea-japan-call-for-complete-denuclearisation-of-north-korea)). On 30 October the U.S., South Korean and Japanese foreign ministers, meeting on the sidelines of the G20 in Atlanta, reaffirmed their "resolute commitment to the complete denuclearisation" of North Korea, according to a U.S. State Department readout (same source).

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung, in office since June 2025, has pursued a more conciliatory posture than his predecessor, pledging in a March 2026 address to formally respect North Korea's political system and to avoid pursuing "unification by absorption," while working to revive stalled inter-Korean dialogue and stabilize relations with China and Japan alongside the U.S. alliance ([The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2026/03/south-korean-president-pledges-to-respect-norths-system-in-march-1st-address/)). In September he suggested that Washington pursue a freeze, rather than immediate elimination, of North Korea's nuclear programme in exchange for sanctions relief; critics say this could implicitly accept North Korea as a nuclear state ([Al Jazeera](https://aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/31/us-south-korea-japan-call-for-complete-denuclearisation-of-north-korea)). North Korea has shown little reciprocal interest in resuming talks and has deepened military and economic cooperation with Russia and China; in May 2026 Beijing and Moscow issued a joint statement opposing further sanctions or military pressure on Pyongyang.

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**Whether unification remains a live goal.** For decades both Korean governments nominally endorsed eventual reunification. North Korea's government, under Kim Jong Un, now explicitly rejects that goal, terming South Korea a permanently hostile foreign state ([KCNA, cited via Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/8/19/kim-jong-un-pledges-to-speed-up-nuclear-build-up-over-us-south-korea-drills)). South Korea's Lee Jae-myung administration has not abandoned the constitutional goal of unification but has in practice de-emphasized it in favor of "peaceful coexistence" and mutual recognition, a shift some conservative South Korean commentators criticize as a concession to Pyongyang, while the administration frames it as realism about near-term prospects.

**Whether North Korea's nuclear arsenal is negotiable.** North Korea's government describes its nuclear status as "irreversible" and a permanent feature of its security posture, not a bargaining chip for sanctions relief ([Al Jazeera, citing KCNA](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/8/19/kim-jong-un-pledges-to-speed-up-nuclear-build-up-over-us-south-korea-drills)). The United States and South Korea's stated policy goal remains the "complete denuclearization" of North Korea, though the 2019 Hanoi summit's collapse over sequencing disputes, and the lack of any denuclearization progress since, has led many analysts to treat that goal as increasingly aspirational rather than a near-term negotiating basis — a gap between declared policy and analyst assessment rather than a resolved dispute.

**Responsibility for renewed tension.** Pyongyang's state media frames its weapons tests and hardened posture as a defensive response to what it calls hostile U.S.–South Korea–Japan joint military exercises and sanctions. Seoul, Washington and Tokyo characterize North Korea's missile and nuclear program as the primary driver of instability, and their joint exercises as defensive deterrence in response to it — each side presents its own actions as reactive to the other's.

**Who planted the mines in the DMZ.** South Korea's military and a joint South Korean–UN investigation attribute the September explosions that injured three South Korean soldiers to mines laid by North Korean forces; Pyongyang rejects the finding and Seoul's demand for an apology, and accuses Seoul of provocation ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/12/north-korea-fires-multiple-ballistic-missiles-towards-the-sea), [AP via NY1](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/ap-top-news/2026/10/23/south-korea-proposes-military-talks-with-north-korea-to-calm-rising-border-tensions0)). The attribution rests on the investigating side's conclusions; no independent assessment was found.
