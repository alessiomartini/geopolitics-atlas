# Yemen Civil War

## Historical Context

Yemen's modern conflict has roots in decades of uneven development between the north and south and the long rule of president Ali Abdullah Saleh, who unified North and South Yemen in 1990 and governed until the 2011 Arab Spring uprisings forced his resignation. The Houthi movement (Ansar Allah), a Zaidi Shia revivalist group based in the northern Saada region, had fought a series of wars against Saleh's government in the 2000s partly in reaction to perceived Saudi religious and financial influence. In September 2014, taking advantage of popular anger over corruption, unemployment and fuel-price rises, and aided opportunistically by their former enemy Saleh, the Houthis seized the capital Sanaa and later forced the internationally recognized government of Abd Rabbu Mansour Hadi into exile ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Yemeni-Civil-War)). In March 2015, alarmed by a Houthi force on its southern border, Saudi Arabia assembled a multinational coalition — including the United Arab Emirates — and began air strikes and a blockade intended to restore Hadi's government, with US and UK logistical and intelligence support. The intervention dramatically escalated the war's toll, triggering mass displacement, the world's largest cholera outbreak on record, and repeated famine warnings. A UN-brokered truce in April 2022 halted major front-line fighting for several years, even after formally lapsing that October, while a separate southern secessionist movement (the Southern Transitional Council) and periodic al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula activity have added further layers to the conflict ([BBC](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/world-middle-east-44466574)).

## Current State of Facts

The de facto ceasefire that had held since 2022 has come under severe strain in 2026. Fighting resumed in Hodeidah governorate in early July 2026, and in early August the Houthis launched missile and drone strikes on Yemeni government military camps in Marib and Hadramout governorates that killed at least 30 government soldiers, described as the deadliest attacks on military sites in years; government forces responded along several front lines ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/6/houthis-claim-to-have-killed-45-in-attacks-on-yemeni-government-forces)). UN Special Envoy for Yemen Hans Grundberg said the escalation had left Yemen at its greatest risk of a return to large-scale conflict since the 2022 truce ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/7/houthi-attacks-on-govt-forces-hint-that-a-major-battle-in-yemen-is-brewing)). Separately, the Houthis resumed missile and drone attacks on Israel and on Red Sea shipping after the February 2026 outbreak of an Israel-US war with Iran, before pausing again following a mid-June 2026 US-Iran memorandum of understanding ([France 24](https://www.france24.com/en/middle-east/20260329-yemen-houthi-attacks-on-israel-open-new-front-mideast-war-threaten-red-sea-shipping)). On the humanitarian side, the UN estimates more than 22 million people in Yemen will need humanitarian assistance in 2026, with roughly 5.2 million people internally displaced, and warns of catastrophic (IPC Phase 5) conditions for tens of thousands absent sustained aid, against a response plan that was only about 29% funded in 2025 ([UN OCHA](https://www.unocha.org/publications/report/yemen/yemen-humanitarian-needs-and-response-plan-2025-january-2025-enar)).

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**Who bears responsibility for civilian casualties.** The UN human rights office has said Saudi-led coalition air strikes caused the largest share of civilian casualties among all parties, with the Yemen Data Project recording tens of thousands of air raids of which roughly a third hit clearly civilian targets. Saudi Arabia and the coalition have rejected this characterization, calling UN reporting "biased" and denying that strikes deliberately targeted civilians, while insisting they take precautions to avoid civilian harm ([BBC](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/world-middle-east-45348364)). The Houthis, for their part, accuse the coalition of war crimes while downplaying their own record of laying landmines, using human shields, and shelling populated areas such as Taiz and, in 2026, Marib's displacement camps.

**Whether the Houthis are an "Iranian proxy."** Saudi Arabia, the Yemeni government, and many US officials describe the Houthis as an Iranian proxy force armed and directed by Tehran, and the US has presented physical evidence of Iranian weapons transfers. Iran and the Houthis themselves deny that the movement is Iranian-directed. A number of academic analysts (including at the Brookings Institution) argue this framing overstates Iranian control: Iranian support was minimal before 2014 and the Houthis retain significant operational autonomy rooted in local Zaidi and tribal politics rather than acting simply on Tehran's orders ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-danger-of-calling-the-houthis-an-iranian-proxy/)).

**Legitimacy of rival governments.** The Saudi- and UAE-backed Presidential Leadership Council in Aden claims to be Yemen's sole legitimate government, recognized by the UN; the Houthi administration in Sanaa, which controls the majority of Yemen's population, claims governing legitimacy over the country from its de facto capital, a claim not recognized internationally.
