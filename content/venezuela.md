# Venezuela Crisis

## Historical Context

Venezuela's crisis combines a prolonged internal political and economic collapse with a long-running external territorial dispute. Domestically, Hugo Chávez's "Bolivarian Revolution" (1999–2013) and his successor Nicolás Maduro's government presided over a steep economic decline after the mid-2010s oil price crash, driven by falling oil production, hyperinflation, and US sanctions imposed from 2017 onward in response to democratic backsliding; the UN estimates roughly 7–8 million Venezuelans left the country over the following decade, one of the largest displacement crises globally. Maduro's re-election in 2018 was widely regarded as neither free nor fair, and the National Assembly's opposition-led speaker, Juan Guaidó, declared himself interim president in 2019 with US and allied backing, though he never gained effective control. Separately, Venezuela has for over a century disputed Guyana's sovereignty over the Essequibo region, roughly two-thirds of Guyana's territory, rich in oil discovered offshore in the 2010s; Venezuela argues an 1899 arbitration award was invalid, while Guyana relies on that award as the established boundary. Guyana brought the boundary dispute to the International Court of Justice in 2018; in December 2023 Maduro held a domestic referendum in which voters backed annexing Essequibo, followed by a 2024 law purporting to formally incorporate the region into Venezuela.

## Current State of Facts

Venezuela's July 2024 presidential election produced a disputed outcome: opposition candidate Edmundo González, backed by Nobel laureate María Corina Machado, published polling-station tallies indicating he won with roughly twice Maduro's vote share, while the Maduro-aligned National Electoral Council declared Maduro the winner without releasing detailed results; the United States, the European Parliament, and several Latin American governments recognized González as president-elect ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2024/11/19/us-recognises-venezuelan-opposition-leader-gonzalez-as-president-elect)). Beginning in September 2025, the US military conducted a campaign of strikes on boats in the Caribbean and Pacific it said were smuggling drugs, killing over 60 people by late October 2025; the UN human rights chief and Human Rights Watch called the strikes unlawful extrajudicial killings, saying no evidence showed the targeted boats posed an imminent threat ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2025/10/1166234); [HRW](https://www.hrw.org/news/2025/12/16/qa-us-military-operations-in-the-caribbean-pacific)). On 3 January 2026, US forces launched "Operation Absolute Resolve," a large air and special-forces operation involving more than 150 aircraft that struck Venezuelan air defenses and captured Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, at their Caracas residence; Maduro was flown to New York and indicted on narco-terrorism, cocaine-trafficking, and weapons charges ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/01/03/nx-s1-5665670/venezuela-strikes-us-maduro)). Venezuela's Supreme Court installed Vice President Delcy Rodríguez as acting president; her government has since opened Venezuela's oil sector to US investment. The ICJ proceeded with oral hearings in the Guyana-Venezuela boundary case in 2026, after earlier ordering Venezuela not to hold elections in the disputed Essequibo territory.

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**Nature of the January 2026 operation.** The US government has described the capture of Maduro as a law-enforcement and counter-narcotics operation executing US narco-trafficking indictments against a wanted criminal, not regime change. Venezuela's government (under Maduro before his capture, and subsequently figures aligned with the former administration) and some regional governments characterized it as an illegal act of war and violation of Venezuelan sovereignty; Venezuela's interior minister said more than 100 people were killed in the operation, a figure not independently verified by international bodies ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/07/us-venezuela-military-operation-maduro-injuries-casualties.html)).

**Legality of the prior boat strikes.** The Trump administration described the September–October 2025 strikes on suspected drug-trafficking vessels as a legitimate armed response to narco-terrorism threatening the United States. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Human Rights Watch, and legal experts at the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights disputed this, arguing the strikes had no clear basis in international law absent evidence of an imminent threat, and characterized them as extrajudicial killings ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/10/6/can-us-strikes-on-suspected-drug-boats-off-venezuela-be-legally-justified)).

**Essequibo sovereignty.** Venezuela's government (across the Maduro and subsequent administrations) maintains the Essequibo region is historically Venezuelan territory, citing an 1899 arbitration it calls fraudulent, and has taken legislative and electoral steps to formalize a claim. Guyana's government, backed by the 1899 award and the bulk of international legal opinion, maintains the boundary is settled and that Venezuela's 2023–2024 referendum and annexation law have no legal effect; the dispute remains before the ICJ, whose jurisdiction Venezuela has separately contested.

**2024 election legitimacy.** Venezuela's National Electoral Council, a body whose leadership is aligned with the ruling party, declared Maduro the winner of the 2024 election. The opposition coalition, most Western governments, and independent electoral analysts who examined the published polling-station tallies concluded González won by a wide margin, a position that shaped subsequent US and allied non-recognition of Maduro's government prior to his capture.
