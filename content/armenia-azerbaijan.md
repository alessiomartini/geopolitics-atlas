# Armenia-Azerbaijan Conflict

## Historical Context

The core of the Armenia-Azerbaijan conflict is Nagorno-Karabakh, a mountainous, majority-ethnic-Armenian enclave that Soviet authorities placed inside Azerbaijan in the 1920s. In 1988, as Soviet control weakened, ethnic Armenians in the region demanded its transfer to Armenia; the dispute escalated into the First Nagorno-Karabakh War (1988–1994) after both Armenia and Azerbaijan gained independence in the Soviet collapse. That war killed an estimated 30,000 people and displaced over a million — ethnic Azerbaijanis from Armenia, Karabakh and surrounding districts, and ethnic Armenians from Azerbaijan proper. It ended with a 1994 Russian-brokered ceasefire that left Karabakh and seven adjacent Azerbaijani districts under the control of a self-declared, internationally unrecognized Armenian-backed republic ("Artsakh").

Azerbaijan, strengthened by oil-fueled military spending, launched the Second Nagorno-Karabakh War in September–November 2020, retaking large parts of Karabakh and the surrounding territories with substantial Turkish backing; a Russian-brokered ceasefire ended that war and deployed Russian peacekeepers to the remaining Armenian-held core of Karabakh. In December 2022, Azerbaijan began blockading the Lachin Corridor, the sole road connecting Karabakh to Armenia, producing a severe humanitarian crisis. In September 2023, Azerbaijan launched a rapid military offensive that overran the remaining Armenian-held territory within about a day; within roughly two weeks, more than 100,000 ethnic Armenians — nearly the entire population of Karabakh — fled to Armenia, and the self-declared Artsakh republic formally dissolved on 1 January 2024.

## Current State of Facts

Since the 2023 exodus, the conflict's center of gravity has shifted from Karabakh itself, now firmly under Azerbaijani control, to bilateral normalization between Yerevan and Baku. Armenia and Azerbaijan initialed the text of a draft bilateral peace treaty on 8 August 2025 at a US-brokered summit that also unveiled the "Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity" (TRIPP) — a US-leased transit corridor through Armenia's Syunik province connecting Azerbaijan to its Nakhchivan exclave, on land that would remain under Armenian jurisdiction ([Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2025/08/08/armenia-azerbaijan-war-peace-deal-trump-white-house-tripp-zangezur-corridor/)). As of mid-2026 the treaty remains initialed but unsigned; Azerbaijan has conditioned final signature on Armenia amending its constitution to remove language interpreted as an implicit territorial claim to Karabakh, a change Armenia's government says it intends to put to voters, with parliamentary elections expected around June 2026 complicating the timeline ([The Central Asia-Caucasus Analyst](https://www.cacianalyst.org/publications/analytical-articles/item/13917-the-armenia-azerbaijan-peace-process-enters-2026.html)).

Border delimitation and demarcation work is proceeding incrementally, moving step by step from the Georgian border south toward Iran; roughly 12–13 kilometers of the 996-kilometer shared frontier — a single segment in the Tavush region completed in 2024 — remained the only formally demarcated section as of the two governments' 13th border-commission meeting in April 2026, with further sections under negotiation ([Caspian News](https://caspiannews.com/news-detail/azerbaijan-armenia-advance-border-delimitation-process-2026-4-30-0/)). The International Crisis Group assesses that despite the diplomatic progress, the peace remains fragile, with unresolved issues including prisoner releases, the fate of Armenian cultural and religious heritage in former Karabakh territory, and mutual distrust ([International Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/brf/europe-central-asia/caucasus/armenian-azerbaijani-conflict/98-armenia-and-azerbaijan-hard-road-lasting-peace)).

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**"Ethnic cleansing" vs. "voluntary departure."** Armenia's government, under Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan, and human-rights organizations including Freedom House have characterized the 2023 exodus of Karabakh's ethnic Armenian population as ethnic cleansing, pointing to the preceding blockade, military assault, and residents' fear of living under Azerbaijani rule. Azerbaijan's government has firmly rejected this framing: presidential foreign-policy adviser Hikmet Hajiyev told AFP that the population's departure was a personal choice rather than a forced expulsion, and Azerbaijan's Foreign Ministry has said the mass movement "has nothing to do with forced relocation," while separately accusing Armenia of having ethnically cleansed Azerbaijanis from the same territories during the First Karabakh War in the early 1990s.

**The Zangezur/TRIPP corridor's status.** Azerbaijan and Turkey have periodically pressed for an extraterritorial corridor across Armenia's Syunik province, free of Armenian customs or security checks, framing it as restoring a historic transit link. Armenia's government and Iran (which borders Syunik and opposes any change to regional transit routes) have resisted this, viewing it as a threat to Armenian sovereignty; the 2025 TRIPP arrangement was structured to keep the corridor under Armenian legal jurisdiction while granting a US company development and lease rights, a compromise both Baku and Yerevan have publicly endorsed but which critics in both countries still contest.

**Responsibility for the wars' outbreak.** Armenian sources emphasize Karabakh Armenians' right to self-determination as the basis for the original 1988 mobilization; Azerbaijani sources, and the government in Baku, describe the Armenian-backed secession and subsequent occupation of Azerbaijani districts outside Karabakh proper (1993–1994) as an illegal occupation later corrected by the 2020 and 2023 offensives, which Azerbaijan frames as restoring its internationally recognized territorial integrity — a position the UN and most states have generally accepted regarding Azerbaijan's borders, even as they raised humanitarian concerns about how the 2023 offensive and exodus were handled.
