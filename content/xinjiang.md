# Xinjiang

## Historical Context

Xinjiang, in China's northwest, is home to roughly 11.5 million Uyghurs, a predominantly Muslim, Turkic-speaking people, alongside Han Chinese and other ethnic groups. The region came under Qing dynasty control in the mid-18th century and was formally incorporated as a province in 1884; it briefly asserted independence as the short-lived "East Turkestan Republics" in the 1930s and 1940s before the Chinese Communist Party took control in 1949 and established the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region in 1955. Periodic unrest, including ethnic riots in Ürümqi in 2009 that killed roughly 200 people, and a series of attacks in the 2010s that Chinese authorities attributed to Uyghur separatist and Islamist militants, preceded a major shift in government policy.

In 2014 Beijing launched the "Strike Hard Campaign Against Violent Terrorism," and from around 2017 the government dramatically expanded a system of internment facilities across Xinjiang. Independent researchers, journalists, and Western governments estimated that up to 1 million or more Uyghurs and other Turkic Muslims were detained in these facilities at the campaign's peak, a figure that traces to 2018 estimates cited by a UN human-rights committee and US officials. Leaked internal Chinese government documents — the 2019 "China Cables" and the 2022 "Xinjiang Police Files," based on hacked police databases obtained by researcher Adrian Zenz and shared with international media consortiums — described detention criteria, surveillance protocols, and use-of-force authorizations inside the camp system, forming a central evidentiary basis for subsequent international scrutiny.

## Current State of Facts

The Chinese government states that its "vocational education and training centers" program in Xinjiang has concluded, and that former detainees have "graduated" into employment; independent verification of current detention numbers is not possible because Xinjiang remains largely closed to unsupervised outside monitoring. In August 2022, the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) released an assessment concluding that China's "arbitrary and discriminatory detention" of Uyghurs and other predominantly Muslim groups may constitute crimes against humanity, and documented concerns including allegations of torture, forced birth control, restrictions on religious practice, and destruction of religious sites ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2022/08/un-human-rights-office-issues-assessment-human-rights-concerns-xinjiang)).

Forced-labor concerns have become the dominant international focus in 2025–2026. In January 2026, independent UN human-rights experts said they were alarmed by continued reports of forced labor transfers of Uyghurs, Tibetans and other minorities across China, citing a regional five-year plan that projected 13.75 million labor-transfer instances in Xinjiang between 2021 and 2025 ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2026/01/un-experts-alarmed-reports-forced-labour-uyghur-tibetan-and-other-minorities)). Under the US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, US Customs and Border Protection has detained over $4.7 billion worth of imports and barred goods from 43 additional companies through 2025 on suspicion of Xinjiang forced-labor links, while the EU's Forced Labour Regulation, adopted in 2024, explicitly flags Xinjiang-linked supply chains ahead of its scheduled 2027 enforcement start ([US Department of Homeland Security](https://www.dhs.gov/2025-updates-strategy-prevent-importation-goods-mined-produced-or-manufactured-forced-labor-peoples)).

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**"Mass internment camps" vs. "vocational training centers."** Human Rights Watch, the OHCHR, and most Western governments (the US, UK, Canada, and others) describe the Xinjiang facilities as a system of arbitrary mass detention targeting Uyghurs and other Muslim minorities based on ethnicity and religious practice. China's State Council Information Office and its embassies describe the same facilities as voluntary "vocational education and training centers" established to teach Mandarin and job skills and to prevent extremism, asserting the program concluded by late 2019 and that graduates found employment; Chinese state media (CGTN, Xinhua, Global Times) has aired testimonials from self-described former trainees who reject the term "camps" and the "genocide" characterization as fabrications by what Beijing calls "anti-China forces." Independent researchers point to satellite imagery analyzed by BBC-commissioned analysts identifying dozens of walled, guarded compounds built after 2017, and to leaked police files describing detention quotas and escape-prevention measures, as evidence contradicting the voluntary-training framing.

**"Genocide" designation.** The United States government (under both the Trump and Biden administrations) and several Western parliaments (including Canada's and the UK's) have formally designated China's policies in Xinjiang as genocide or as consistent with the legal definition of genocide, citing documented forced sterilization and birth-control measures alongside mass detention. China's government rejects the genocide designation outright, calling it, in the words of its Geneva mission, "the lie of the century," and disputes the underlying demographic and sterilization claims, which it attributes primarily to the German researcher Adrian Zenz, whom Beijing has sanctioned and accuses of fabricating data; a number of independent demographers have also debated the precision of Zenz's specific birth-rate calculations, even where they do not dispute the broader pattern of detention.

**Scale of detention.** The oft-cited figure of "up to 1 million or more" detained originates from 2018 estimates relayed by a UN racial-discrimination committee member and repeated by US officials and researchers; because Xinjiang restricts independent access, no organization has produced a verified current count, and estimates have not been meaningfully updated with primary evidence since the 2019–2022 leaks. China disputes the figure entirely, providing no alternative verified count of its own.
