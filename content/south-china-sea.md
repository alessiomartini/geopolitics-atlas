# South China Sea Disputes

## Historical Context

The South China Sea is claimed in overlapping and competing ways by China, Taiwan, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia and Brunei, covering waters believed to hold significant fisheries and undersea hydrocarbon resources and through which a large share of global shipping passes. China's claim is anchored in a "nine-dash line" — a vaguely defined boundary first depicted on Chinese maps in the late 1940s — encompassing roughly 90 percent of the sea and overlapping the exclusive economic zones that Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia and Brunei claim under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS).

In 2013 the Philippines brought a case against China before an arbitral tribunal at the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) under UNCLOS. On 12 July 2016, the tribunal ruled that China's nine-dash line and its claimed "historic rights" within it had no basis in international law, that several of China's land reclamation and enforcement activities in Philippine waters were unlawful, and that none of the disputed Spratly features generated an exclusive economic zone ([PCA press release](https://pcacases.com/web/sendAttach/1801)). China refused to participate in the arbitration and rejected the ruling as "null and void," a position it has maintained since. Since 2002, ASEAN and China have discussed a "Code of Conduct" to manage the dispute, but negotiations have advanced slowly.

## Current State of Facts

Confrontations between China and the Philippines have continued through 2026, concentrated at Second Thomas Shoal — where the grounded Philippine vessel BRP Sierra Madre anchors a small military garrison — and at Scarborough Shoal. On 20 July 2026, a Philippine Navy sailor was injured when Chinese coast guard personnel struck him with a wooden baton during a confrontation near Second Thomas Shoal; the two governments blamed each other for the incident ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/21/china-and-philippines-trade-accusations-after-sailor-injured-at-sea)). Days later, on 23–24 July 2026, Chinese coast guard vessels fired water cannon at Philippine government boats near Scarborough Shoal on successive days, prompting Manila to summon China's ambassador ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/24/china-philippine-coastguard-vessels-clash-in-south-china-sea)). In August 2026 the U.S. State Department criticized a Chinese-designated "nature reserve" around Scarborough Shoal as an effort to entrench control and restrict Filipino fishermen's access ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/8/us-slams-chinese-nature-reserve-as-effort-to-claim-south-china-sea)).

In July 2026, marking the tenth anniversary of the PCA ruling, a group of roughly a dozen countries including the United States, Japan, Australia and the Philippines issued a joint statement calling on China to comply with the award; China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs rejected the statement, restating its sovereignty claim over the South China Sea islands and "historic rights" in the area ([China Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/xw/wjbxw/202607/t20260712_11980357.html)). ASEAN, chaired by the Philippines in 2026, has continued pushing to conclude a binding Code of Conduct, though a key sticking point remains China's preference for a non-binding framework versus ASEAN's push for legal force.

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**Legal basis of China's claim.** China's government maintains it holds "historic rights" and sovereignty over the South China Sea islands and their surrounding waters based on centuries of asserted use, a position it and state media such as CGTN present as backed by "undeniable historical records" ([CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-07-13/Historical-records-Tracing-China-s-sovereignty-in-the-South-China-Sea-1OKtf8b9E88/p.html)). The Philippines, the other claimant states, and the 2016 PCA tribunal hold that UNCLOS — which China has ratified — supersedes historic-rights claims where they are not compatible with the convention's zone system, and that the tribunal's ruling settled the legal question in the Philippines' favor ([PCA press release](https://pcacases.com/web/sendAttach/1801)). China's rejection of the tribunal's jurisdiction means the two positions remain formally unresolved between the parties, even though most non-claimant Tier A/B/C observers treat the ruling as authoritative.

**Who is the aggressor in shoal confrontations.** Manila and most Western governments describe Chinese coast guard actions — water cannon use, ramming, and the baton incident at Second Thomas Shoal — as coercive escalation against lawful Philippine resupply and patrol activity within its exclusive economic zone. Beijing's foreign ministry and coast guard describe their own actions as lawful "rights protection" and law enforcement against Philippine vessels it says are trespassing in Chinese-administered waters, and has in past incidents accused Philippine personnel of provoking confrontations.

**Role of outside powers.** The U.S., Japan and Australia frame their naval patrols and statements in the region as defense of freedom of navigation and international law. China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs characterizes the same activity, plus U.S.-Philippines joint exercises, as external interference by non-claimant states seeking to contain China — a framing it applied explicitly to Japan's July 2026 statement on the arbitration ruling, saying Japan is "not a party in the South China Sea" and "in no position to pass judgment on China's territorial sovereignty" ([China Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/xw/wjbxw/202607/t20260712_11980357.html)).
