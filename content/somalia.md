# Somalia / Al-Shabaab Insurgency

## Historical Context

Al-Shabaab ("the youth") emerged from the collapse of the Islamic Courts Union (ICU), a network of Sharia courts that had unified much of southern Somalia and briefly seized Mogadishu in 2006 after defeating a US-backed warlord coalition. Alarmed by the ICU's rise on its border, Ethiopia invaded in December 2006 with US backing and toppled it within weeks, restoring Somalia's internationally recognized but weak Transitional Federal Government ([South African History Online](https://sahistory.org.za/article/history-al-shabaab)). Al-Shabaab, the ICU's hardline youth militia, capitalized on Somali resentment of the Ethiopian occupation to launch an insurgency, formally aligning with al-Qaeda in 2012. The African Union Mission in Somalia (AMISOM), authorized by the UN Security Council and deployed from 2007, gradually pushed Al-Shabaab out of Mogadishu and other major towns by 2011–2012, but the group retained control of large rural areas of south-central Somalia and continued a campaign of bombings, assassinations and mass-casualty attacks, including the 2013 Westgate Mall and 2017 Mogadishu truck-bomb attacks. AMISOM was succeeded by the African Union Transition Mission (ATMIS) and, in 2025, by the African Union Support and Stabilization Mission in Somalia (AUSSOM), reflecting a long, unresolved effort to build up Somali federal forces capable of holding territory independently.

## Current State of Facts

Somalia's federal government, with Ugandan troops under AUSSOM, launched Operation Rolling Thunder in March 2026, targeting Al-Shabaab strongholds in the Lower Shabelle region, and President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud announced plans for a further offensive phase in July 2026 ([Dawan Africa](https://www.dawan.africa/news/somalia-plans-new-offensive-against-al-shabaab)). Despite periodic government gains, the International Crisis Group assesses the conflict as having settled back into a long-standing "managed conflict" or stalemate: after Al-Shabaab's 2022–2023 losses, its 2025 counter-offensives reversed many government gains and re-established territorial control roughly comparable to pre-2022 levels, including pressure around Mogadishu ([International Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/brf/africa/somalia/b212-new-chapter-same-stalemate-somalias-war-al-shabaab)). Al-Shabaab continues to conduct ambushes and attacks on government convoys, including a May 2026 ambush near Baidoa that killed senior military commanders. Separately, Somalia faces an intensifying humanitarian crisis: after four consecutive failed rainy seasons, the federal government declared a national drought emergency in November 2025, with UN OCHA estimating at least 4.4 million people facing high levels of acute food insecurity and roughly 500,000 people displaced by drought, compounding conflict-driven displacement, amid a 2025 response plan only about 24% funded ([UN OCHA](https://www.unocha.org/publications/report/somalia/somalia-2025-drought-emergency-situation-report-no-1-27-november-2025)).

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**Government claims of an imminent Al-Shabaab defeat.** Somali federal officials have periodically stated that Al-Shabaab is nearing military defeat or has been forced toward surrender negotiations, framing offensives since 2022 as decisive turning points. Independent analysts, including the International Crisis Group and the Combating Terrorism Center at West Point, are skeptical, arguing that neither side can achieve a decisive victory: government-held gains have repeatedly proven fragile, hampered by desertions among newly trained soldiers, clan-militia (Ma'awisley) reliability problems, and corruption, while Al-Shabaab retains the capacity to reclaim lost villages and mount coordinated attacks near the capital ([CTC West Point](https://ctc.westpoint.edu/somalias-stalled-offensive-against-al-shabaab-taking-stock-of-obstacles/)).

**Extent of Al-Shabaab's territorial control.** Precise control maps are disputed because Al-Shabaab operates as both a territorial administrator in areas it firmly holds and a "shadow" tax-collecting authority — including checkpoints and levies — across much wider areas it does not directly govern, making government and researcher claims about "liberated" territory difficult to verify independently; the EU Asylum Agency and researchers such as the Hiraal Institute explicitly caveat their control maps as approximate ([EUAA](https://www.euaa.europa.eu/coi/somalia/2025/security-situation/12-armed-actors-and-relevant-developments/122-al-shabaab-control-areas-presence-and-influence)).

**Root causes of the insurgency's persistence.** Somali and Western officials often frame the conflict primarily as counterterrorism against a jihadist group. Some regional analysts argue this understates how Al-Shabaab exploits clan grievances, weak governance, and taxation of local trade to sustain itself, meaning military pressure alone — without political and governance reform — is unlikely to end the insurgency.
