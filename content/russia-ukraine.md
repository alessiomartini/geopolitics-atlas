# Russia-Ukraine War

## Historical Context

Russia and Ukraine share centuries of intertwined history, but the current war's roots lie in the 1991 Soviet collapse, after which Ukraine became independent within its Soviet-era borders, including Crimea and the Donbas. Tensions sharpened after Ukraine's 2013–2014 Euromaidan protests toppled a pro-Russian president; Russia responded by annexing Crimea in March 2014 following a referendum held under Russian military presence and with no international observers, a move the UN General Assembly declared invalid by a 100–11 vote ([UN press release](https://press.un.org/en/2014/ga11493.doc.htm)). Russia also backed separatist forces that seized parts of Donetsk and Luhansk, triggering a war that the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission estimated had killed 14,200–14,400 people by the end of 2021, despite the 2014–2015 Minsk agreements meant to freeze it ([UN OHCHR/OCHA](https://ukraine.un.org/en/168060-conflict-related-civilian-casualties-ukraine)).

Through 2021, Russia built up troop numbers near Ukraine's borders, eventually reaching roughly 190,000 personnel, while President Vladimir Putin publicly questioned Ukraine's legitimacy as a state and demanded binding guarantees that Ukraine would never join NATO. On 21 February 2022 Putin recognized the separatist "republics" in Donetsk and Luhansk, and on 24 February 2022 he launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, which he termed a "special military operation" aimed at Ukraine's "demilitarization and denazification." The invasion was condemned by most of the international community and triggered sweeping Western sanctions on Russia, large-scale military aid to Ukraine, and the largest European refugee movement since World War II.

## Current State of Facts

As of August 2026 the war is in its fifth year and is broadly described by analysts as a grinding stalemate along a front line where movement is now measured in individual villages rather than large territorial shifts; neither side currently appears capable of a decisive breakthrough. Russia controls roughly 18–19% of internationally recognized Ukrainian territory, including Crimea (occupied since 2014), most of Luhansk oblast, and parts of Donetsk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson oblasts, while Kharkiv, Kupiansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson city remain under sustained Ukrainian control under continued Russian pressure.

(The 18-19% territorial-control share and the general stalemate description above date from August 2026 and were not re-verified; no map-based control figure could be confirmed in November 2026. What was checked in November is the recent direction of movement, from the Institute for the Study of War (ISW, published via Critical Threats; tier C, US think tank). ISW's assessment of 1 November 2026 estimates that in October 2026 Russian forces gained or consolidated about 139.85 km2 and lost about 167.14 km2, a net loss of 27.29 km2 (76.38 km2 if infiltration areas are counted), and that Russian gains occurred only in Sumy and Zaporizhzhia oblasts. ISW says Russia holds the initiative around Dobropillia (Donetsk oblast) but with only marginal gains, Ukraine holds it in the Kupiansk, Lyman and Oleksandrivka directions, and that in Kostiantynivka Russian forces have advanced into about 36% of the town and infiltrated about 76% of it without seizing it ([ISW, 1 November](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-november-1-2026)). ISW's 8 November assessment reports no confirmed Russian gains near Lyman, Sloviansk, Kostiantynivka or Kupiansk on 7-8 November, some Russian advances or infiltration around Dobropillia, and presents as Zelensky's claim, not verified, that Ukraine has retaken more than 800 km2 in the Oleksandrivka direction in 2026 ([ISW, 8 November](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-november-8-2026)). These are one analyst's estimates; ISW's method of counting infiltration areas is its own and other trackers (e.g. DeepStateMap) could not be read for comparison. Fighting continues to inflict heavy civilian and military harm. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine recorded (for the first half of 2026) 1,396 civilians killed and 7,978 injured in the first half of 2026 alone — a 37% increase over the same period in 2025 — driven largely by long-range missile and drone strikes and by short-range drones near the front line ([OHCHR](https://ukraine.ohchr.org/en/Civilian-Casualties-Soar-in-Ukraine-in-the-First-Half-of-2026-Amid-Escalating-Attacks-and-Intensifying-Use-of-Deadly-Weapons-UN-Human-Rights-Monitors-Say)). Civilian harm has risen further since mid-year: the UN mission verified at least 328 civilians killed and 2,105 injured in September 2026 — 85% more than in September 2025 (249 killed, 1,068 injured) — with casualties recorded in Kyiv on 22 of 30 days, and its head said the first two weeks of October were "among the deadliest periods for civilians in years," citing attacks on Pryluky (22 killed, 45 injured on 6–7 October), Kramatorsk (8 October), Zaporizhzhia (10 October) and Lutsk (14 October) ([OHCHR](https://ukraine.ohchr.org/en/Civilian-casualties-remain-at-record-levels-amid-intensifying-attacks-on-energy-as-winter-approaches)). The mission links the rise to renewed systematic Russian strikes on energy infrastructure ahead of winter; strikes on 13–14 October temporarily left about 600,000 people in Kyiv without electricity, according to Ukrainian authorities (same source). 

Combined Russian and Ukrainian military casualties (killed, wounded and missing) are estimated by defense analysts to have surpassed 2 million since 2022 ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/02/world/russia-war-casualities-ukraine-2-million-intl)). US-mediated peace talks involving Russian and Ukrainian officials were held in Abu Dhabi and Geneva in early 2026, achieving a prisoner exchange and short, partially-violated Easter and Victory Day truces, but no breakthrough on the core territorial dispute — chiefly Russia's demand to formally retain Crimea, Luhansk, Donetsk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/09/europe/russia-ukraine-escalation-aerial-strikes-intl-vis)). As of late October 2026 there is still no deal: President Trump's "energy ceasefire," announced 11 October, has produced no meaningful result; US envoy Steve Witkoff met Putin on 21 October; and Putin said on Russian television that Moscow is ready to resume US-mediated talks with Kyiv in November, aiming at lasting agreements rather than "a temporary ceasefire." Zelensky says Ukraine is ready for a ceasefire but needs strong security guarantees ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/23/russia-ukraine-strikes-peace-talks.html)). Those talks then took place: as of 14 November 2026, US-mediated trilateral talks between US, Russian and Ukrainian delegations were held in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November, the first direct negotiations reported since February. Euronews reports that US envoy Jared Kushner said Washington had narrowed the gaps (without details) and that Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the talks "very productive"; Zelensky called them "constructive and quite substantive" and said the sides were considering prisoner exchanges of 1,000 or even 1,500 each, which Euronews says had not been officially agreed. No ceasefire or settlement had been announced, Moscow rejects an immediate ceasefire, and Russia demands that Ukraine withdraw from eastern regions it still holds, while Trump has said Ukraine may end up with "a little bit different shape" ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/11/ukraine-russia-peace-talks-enter-second-day-with-territory-and-security-guarantees-in-focu)). European officials consulted separately in Abu Dhabi and were not part of the trilateral format (same source). ISW's 8 November assessment does not mention the talks. Outcome of the second day and any later round could not be verified for this pass (single outlet; Washington Post, CNN and Anadolu items were seen only as search snippets or were blocked). Overnight on 22–23 October Russian strikes on energy facilities in six regions cut power to more than 500,000 people, per Ukraine's Energy Ministry, while Russia's Defense Ministry said it downed 443 Ukrainian drones (same source).

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**Who bears responsibility for the war.** The Ukrainian government, most Western governments, and the majority of international-relations scholarship describe the 2022 invasion as unprovoked aggression driven by Putin's imperial ambitions and denial of Ukraine's separate national identity. A minority of Western scholars, former diplomats and some Russian officials argue that NATO's post-Cold War eastward expansion, and the prospect of Ukrainian membership specifically, created the security dynamic that provoked Moscow's action; NATO Secretary-General Jens Stoltenberg acknowledged that Putin cited NATO's refusal to rule out Ukrainian membership as a stated justification, without endorsing that justification as valid. Critics of the "NATO provocation" framing note it echoes a narrative long promoted by Russian state media (TASS, RT) and that Ukraine was not close to NATO accession in 2021–2022.

**Territorial control claims.** At the Abu Dhabi talks Zelensky said Russian negotiators claimed Moscow had captured half of the Ukrainian-held Donbas, which Kyiv rejected, saying it had video evidence; Zelensky also said NATO's Supreme Allied Commander Europe, Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, had confirmed that Ukrainian battlefield maps were closer to reality. Both are Zelensky's account as reported by Euronews, not independently confirmed ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/11/ukraine-russia-peace-talks-enter-second-day-with-territory-and-security-guarantees-in-focu)). Russian state media and the Russian Ministry of Defense routinely claim faster and broader advances than independent open-source trackers (such as ISW and DeepState) verify; Ukrainian officials likewise sometimes overstate the durability of recaptured ground. Independent, satellite- and geolocation-based front-line trackers are the most reliable arbiter, though even they lag real-time control by days.

**Casualty figures.** Both governments understate their own military losses and, at times, overstate the other side's: Russia's Ministry of Defense has periodically denied or minimized reports of Russian losses, while Ukrainian officials have been reluctant to publish comprehensive casualty totals. Independent Western estimates (e.g., from CSIS and former NATO commanders) of Russian losses running into the hundreds of thousands of dead are the most commonly cited figures in Western reporting, but are themselves derived from intelligence assessments rather than confirmed body counts and vary considerably between institutions.

**"Denazification" claim.** Putin's framing of the invasion as targeting a "Nazi" Ukrainian government, given that Ukraine has a democratically elected Jewish president, is treated by Western governments and mainstream historians as a propagandistic justification rather than an evidence-based claim, and is reported here only as an attributed Russian government position, not as fact.
