# Ethiopia (Tigray and Amhara Conflicts)

## Historical Context

The Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) dominated Ethiopia's ruling coalition for nearly three decades until reformist prime minister Abiy Ahmed, who took office in 2018, sidelined the party while restructuring the coalition and pursuing peace with Eritrea — for which he won the 2019 Nobel Peace Prize. Relations between Abiy's government and the TPLF, which retreated to govern Tigray regionally, deteriorated sharply. War broke out in November 2020 after the federal government accused the TPLF of attacking a federal military base in Tigray; Ethiopian federal forces, allied Eritrean troops, and Amhara regional militias (including "Fano" irregulars, who claimed disputed western Tigray territory) then launched a joint offensive into the region ([International Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/brf/africa/ethiopia-eritrea/b210-ethiopia-eritrea-and-tigray-powder-keg-horn-africa)). The two-year war caused mass civilian casualties, widespread sexual violence, and a man-made famine in Tigray before a November 2022 Pretoria peace agreement between the federal government and the TPLF ended major hostilities. The peace deal left unresolved fault lines: Eritrean troops did not fully withdraw from Tigrayan territory, western Tigray remained under Amhara regional administration and militia control, and Abiy's 2023 move to disband regional special forces (folding them into the federal army) triggered a separate, still-ongoing insurgency by Amhara Fano militias, who felt the move left their region exposed.

## Current State of Facts

Fighting between Ethiopian federal forces and TPLF-aligned units broke out in western Tigray in late January 2026, described by observers as the most serious violation of the 2022 Pretoria agreement to date, with Addis Ababa and the TPLF trading blame over responsibility ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/1/fighting-breaks-out-in-western-tigray-as-ethiopia-and-tplf-trade-blame)). Separately, Amhara Fano militias have sustained an intensifying offensive against federal forces since March 2026, with ACLED recording their monthly attack rate more than 50% higher in March–May 2026 than in the preceding quarter, disrupting government efforts to organize planned federal elections ([Critical Threats Project](https://www.criticalthreats.org/analysis/ethiopia-fano-tplf-abiy-somalia-isis-puntland-drc-m23-rwanda-rubaya-africa-file-may-28-2026)). Ethiopia's federal government has accused Eritrea and Amhara militia elements of backing the TPLF, an alignment analysts describe as a striking reversal of the wartime alliance between Addis Ababa, Asmara and Amhara forces against Tigray ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/7/northern-ethiopias-shifting-alliances-explained)). The war has since widened sharply. Ethiopia held national elections on 1 June 2026: according to results released on 21 June, Abiy Ahmed's Prosperity Party won 438 seats, about 90% of those with announced results, with no voting in Tigray and in at least eight of Amhara's 138 constituencies; Reuters noted that opposition parties accuse the government of arresting their leaders, which the government denies, and that it had been unable to report from inside Ethiopia since mid-February ([Reuters via The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/world/2026/06/21/ethiopian-prime-minister039s-party-wins-parliamentary-majority-election-results-show)). On 23 September 2026 fighting between federal forces and the Tigrayan forces resumed in earnest after weeks of lower-level clashes and federal drone strikes, spreading into Afar and Amhara; the TPLF and six other armed groups, including the Amhara Fano National Movement and the Oromo Liberation Army, announced the "Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Alliance for Survival", which aims to remove Abiy's government ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/24/fighting-widens-across-ethiopia-as-tigray-clashes-escalate)). On 3 October federal forces and allied Tigray Peace Force fighters entered the Tigrayan capital, Mekelle, after the TPLF withdrew, saying its administration was "temporarily moved out" and that it would continue fighting from other positions ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/5/ethiopias-forces-retake-mekelle-amid-renewed-tigray-fighting); [AFP via EWN](https://www.ewn.co.za/ethiopia-controls-tigray-capital-as-regional-rivals-meet-in-egypt/)). Witnesses told AFP the rebels had also lost Shire and Adigrat ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/9/ethiopia-and-eritrea-accuse-one-another-of-launching-war)). By 23 October Critical Threats reported a fluid front in which federal forces were still clearing central Tigray (reporting the capture of Abiy Addi and Zana on 22 October) while the TPLF's Tigray Defence Forces had counterattacked and reportedly retaken a border town the army had vacated; most of these reports are unconfirmed, and no combined casualty totals exist ([Critical Threats](https://www.criticalthreats.org/briefs/ethiopia-war-security-review/october-23-2026)). Diplomatically, Ethiopia closed its embassy in Asmara and expelled ten Eritrean diplomats, after which Eritrea severed relations. The African Union and IGAD have called for de-escalation, but no ceasefire or negotiation process was reported in the sources read. Humanitarian conditions across Amhara, Tigray, Afar and Oromia remain severe: aid access in Amhara is restricted by ongoing conflict and kidnappings targeting aid workers, and IOM tracked 3.3 million internally displaced people nationwide, with Tigray and Amhara hosting the largest shares of returnees, many displaced for three years or more ([ACAPS](https://www.acaps.org/en/countries/ethiopia)).

## Contested Narratives / Common Myths

**"Genocide" claims by both Tigrayan and Amhara advocates.** Some Tigrayan officials, researchers, and diaspora groups describe the 2020–2022 campaign against Tigray — including mass killings, sexual violence and the blockade that produced famine conditions — as genocide, a framing echoed by some genocide-monitoring organizations. Separately, Amhara Fano leaders and nationalist commentators describe federal military operations against Fano since 2023, alongside earlier ethnically targeted killings of Amhara civilians in Oromia, as a "genocide" against Amhara people and accuse Abiy's government of tolerating or enabling the violence. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International, while documenting extensive atrocities by multiple parties, have generally used the narrower legal terms "crimes against humanity" and "ethnic cleansing" rather than endorsing genocide determinations for either side, and no international court has issued a genocide finding on either conflict ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org/report/2022/04/06/we-will-erase-you-land/crimes-against-humanity-and-ethnic-cleansing-ethiopias)).

**Who started the 2026 fighting, and Eritrea's role.** Each side blames the other for the September escalation. Getachew Reda, an adviser to Abiy, blamed the TPLF for starting the attack, while the Tigray Army said the federal government launched a "full-scale offensive" and TPLF vice president Amanuel Assefa spoke of "full-blown war"; the army said it killed at least 272 TPLF fighters in North Wollo, a figure that Al Jazeera did not independently verify ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/24/fighting-widens-across-ethiopia-as-tigray-clashes-escalate); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/24/ethiopian-army-says-272-tigrayan-fighters-killed-as-conflict-escalates)). Ethiopia's foreign minister wrote to the UN Security Council describing an "all-out invasion" by Eritrean troops advancing up to 60 km into Tigray and alleging collusion with the TPLF, and the federal government also names Sudan and Egypt as backers; Eritrea's information minister calls this an "unprovoked war of aggression" aimed at seizing the port of Assab, and Eritrea, Sudan and Egypt deny backing the rebels. AFP reported witnesses seeing Eritrean troops in Adigrat before Ethiopian drone strikes, but Critical Threats' 23 October brief reported no Eritrean deployments, so the claim remains contested ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/9/ethiopia-and-eritrea-accuse-one-another-of-launching-war); [Critical Threats](https://www.criticalthreats.org/briefs/ethiopia-war-security-review/october-23-2026)).

**Western Tigray's status.** Amhara regional authorities and Fano militias assert historical claim to western Tigray (annexed to Tigray region under Ethiopia's ethnic-federal system in the 1990s) and have controlled it since 2020. The TPLF and Tigrayan officials say the area was forcibly and illegally annexed through an ethnic-cleansing campaign that expelled hundreds of thousands of Tigrayans, a characterization corroborated by HRW and Amnesty International documentation of expulsion orders and violence; Fano representatives deny targeting civilians or conducting ethnic cleansing, a denial rights groups say is contradicted by their own field documentation.

**Death toll of the Tigray war.** Estimates vary widely: African Union peace envoy Olusegun Obasanjo cited a figure of roughly 600,000 dead; a University of Ghent research group tracking mortality more conservatively estimated 300,000–400,000 civilian deaths from violence, starvation and lack of healthcare between November 2020 and the Pretoria agreement. Both figures are estimates rather than confirmed counts, given restricted access to the region during the war ([Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/world/article-tigray-war-has-seen-up-to-half-a-million-dead-from-violence-and/)).
